Look at this sentence: "The ninja stole my sandwich." Can you identify which word is the subject? Which is the verb? How are the words related to each other? This is what parsing helps us understand!

What is Parsing?

Parsing is the process of analyzing a string of symbols (words) according to the rules of a formal grammar. It determines the syntactic structure of a sentence by identifying relationships between words.
Two Main Approaches: Constituency Parsing (phrase structure) and Dependency Parsing (word relationships)
Applications: Machine translation, question answering, information extraction, sentiment analysis

Key Challenges in Parsing:

• Ambiguity: "I saw her fly" - is "fly" a noun (insect) or verb (action)?
• Complex sentences with multiple clauses
• Different languages have different structures
• Computational complexity of parsing algorithms

Introduction to Parsing

Understanding sentence structure and relationships

What is Parsing?

Breaking down sentences to understand how words work together

Simple Explanation:

Parsing = Figuring out how words in a sentence connect to each other
Like a detective solving how words work together to make meaning!

Constituency Parsing

Groups words into phrases (like building blocks)

📝 What it does:

Shows how words form groups (phrases), and how phrases form bigger groups

🔤 Important Labels:
NP = Noun Phrase
A group of words that acts like a noun
Examples: "the ninja", "my sandwich", "a dancing robot"
VP = Verb Phrase
A group of words with a verb and what it does
Examples: "stole my lunch", "danced wildly", "flew away"
PP = Prepositional Phrase
A preposition + noun phrase (shows location/time)
Examples: "at the party", "in the kitchen", "on the moon"
💡 Example:
"The ninja stole my sandwich"

• "The ninja" = NP (Noun Phrase)
• "stole my sandwich" = VP (Verb Phrase)
• "my sandwich" = NP (inside the VP)
• "my" = determiner describing "sandwich"

Dependency Parsing

Shows which word connects to which word

📝 What it does:

Shows direct connections: which word modifies or depends on which other word

🔗 Key Concepts:
Word Relationships
Shows how words connect
Example: "ninja" is the subject of "stole"
Head-Dependent
Each word depends on one main word
Example: "The" depends on "ninja"
Relation Labels
Describes the connection type
Example: subject, object, determiner
💡 Example:
"The ninja stole my sandwich"

• "stole" = main word (root)
• "ninja" → depends on "stole" (subject)
• "The" → depends on "ninja" (describes it)
• "sandwich" → depends on "stole" (what was stolen)
• "my" → depends on "sandwich" (describes it)

Where we use parsing in real life

Why Do We Need Parsing?

  • Understand Structure: Know which words describe which other words
  • Solve Confusion: "I saw her fly" - is fly an insect or an action? Parsing helps decide!
  • Find Information: Extract who did what to whom (e.g., "The ninja stole my sandwich" → Subject: ninja, Verb: stole, Object: sandwich)
  • Better AI: Makes translation, Q&A, and text understanding work better

Context-Free Grammars (CFG)

Formal rules for sentence structure

Grammar Rules (CFG)

The recipe book for building sentences

What is CFG?

Context-Free Grammar (CFG) = Rules that tell us how to build sentences
Like a recipe: "A sentence needs a noun phrase + verb phrase"

The Rules

📋 Basic Rules:
• Sentence = Noun Phrase + Verb Phrase
• Noun Phrase = Determiner + Noun
• Verb Phrase = Verb + (maybe Prepositional Phrase)
• Prepositional Phrase = Preposition + Noun Phrase
💡 Example:
"The robot danced"
Sentence = [NP "The robot"] + [VP "danced"]

The Problem: Ambiguity

🤔 What is Ambiguity?

Sometimes one sentence can be understood in multiple ways!

💡 Example:
"I saw her fly"

Could mean:
1. I saw [her fly] → fly = insect (noun) 🪰
2. I saw her [fly] → fly = action (verb) ✈️

Constituency Parsing

Building phrase structure trees

Constituency Parsing in Detail

Building sentence trees step by step

How It Works:

Constituency parsing builds a tree showing how words group into phrases, and phrases into bigger phrases.
Think of it like Russian nesting dolls - smaller groups inside bigger groups!

The Process

📝 Steps:
1. Find small groups (words → phrases)
2. Find bigger groups (phrases → bigger phrases)
3. Build the tree from bottom to top
4. Label each group (NP, VP, PP, etc.)
💡 Simple Example:
"The angry robot"
Words: "The" + "angry" + "robot"
→ Group: Noun Phrase (NP)

Tree Structure

🌳 What You Get:

A tree diagram showing:
• Which words belong together
• How phrases are nested
• The complete sentence structure

       │      │
    NP      VP
"The ninja" "stole"

Why Use Constituency Parsing?

  • See the Big Picture: Understand how entire phrases work together
  • Find Phrases: Identify complete phrases, not just individual words
  • Grammar Rules: Follows traditional grammar structure
  • Easy to Visualize: Tree diagrams are easy to understand

Dependency Parsing

Identifying word-to-word relationships

Dependency Parsing in Detail

Finding direct connections between words

How It Works:

Dependency parsing finds direct relationships between words.
Each word (except one) depends on another word - like a family tree!

Key Concepts

🔗 The Root:

One main word (usually the verb) that everything else connects to

🔗 Dependencies:

Each word points to the word it depends on (modifies or relates to)

🏷️ Labels:

Each connection has a label (subject, object, determiner, etc.)

Common Labels

nsubj = subject
Example: "ninja" in "The ninja stole"
dobj = direct object
Example: "sandwich" in "I ate the sandwich"
det = determiner
Example: "The" in "The ninja"
prep = preposition
Example: "my" in "stole my sandwich"
📚 Complete Example: "The ninja stole my sandwich" Dependency Structure: 
                  stole (ROOT)
                    ╱         ╲
                   │           │
            ninja (nsubj)  sandwich (dobj)
              ╱                    ╱
             │                    │
        The (det)            my (det)
Key Point: "stole" is the root. Everything else connects to it or to words connected to it!

Why Use Dependency Parsing?

  • Direct Relationships: See exactly which word connects to which
  • Simple Structure: Easier to understand than phrase trees
  • Find Roles: Quickly identify subject, object, modifiers
  • Works for Many Languages: Good for languages with different word orders

Practical Examples & Tools

Using NLTK and spaCy for parsing

Tools for Parsing

Python libraries that make parsing easy

Popular Tools:

There are great Python libraries that can parse sentences for you!
You don't need to write all the rules yourself - these tools do it automatically.

spaCy

✅ Best For:

• Dependency parsing (very fast!)
• Production applications
• Easy to use

💡 Simple Example:
import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
doc = nlp("The robot danced")
# Get dependencies!

NLTK

✅ Best For:

• Learning and education
• Constituency parsing
• Many NLP tools

💡 Simple Example:
import nltk
from nltk.parse import *
# Parse sentences!
📚 Quick Comparison: spaCy: ✅ Very fast ✅ Great for dependency parsing ✅ Easy to use ✅ Production-ready ❌ Less control over parsing NLTK: ✅ Great for learning ✅ Many parsing options ✅ Good for constituency parsing ❌ Slower than spaCy ❌ More complex setup Which to Choose? • Learning: Start with NLTK • Real projects: Use spaCy • Both are free and open source!

Getting Started:

  • Install: pip install spacy or pip install nltk
  • Download models: Both need language models (English, etc.)
  • Try examples: Start with simple sentences like "The robot danced"
  • Practice: Parse different sentences to see how it works!

Parsing Evaluation Metrics

Measuring parse quality and accuracy

How Good is Your Parser?

Measuring if parsing is correct

Why Evaluate?

We need to check if our parser is working correctly!
We compare what the parser found with what the correct answer should be.

For Dependency Parsing

📊 UAS (Unlabeled Attachment Score):

How many words are connected to the correct parent word?
Example: 90% UAS = 90% of connections are correct

📊 LAS (Labeled Attachment Score):

How many words have both correct parent AND correct label?
Example: 85% LAS = 85% have correct connection + label

For Constituency Parsing

📊 F1 Score:

How many phrases match the correct answer?
Example: 88% F1 = 88% of phrases are correct

📊 Exact Match:

Is the entire tree exactly correct?
Example: 70% exact match = 70% of sentences are perfect

📚 Simple Example: Sentence: "The robot danced" Correct Dependency: 
      danced (ROOT)
        ╱     ╲
       │       │
  robot (nsubj) The (det)
                │
            connects to "robot"
Parser Found: 
      danced (ROOT) ✅
        ╱     ╲
       │       │
  robot (nsubj) ✅ The (det) ✅
                │
            connects to "robot" ✅
Result: 100% UAS and 100% LAS (perfect!) If parser said "The" → "danced" instead: 
      danced (ROOT)
        ╱     ╲
       │       │
  robot (nsubj) The (det) ❌
                │
            WRONG! Should connect to "robot"
            ❌ Wrong parent = Lower UAS
            ❌ Wrong connection = Lower LAS

What Makes a Good Parser?

  • High Scores: UAS/LAS above 90% is very good!
  • Consistent: Works well on different types of sentences
  • Fast: Parses sentences quickly
  • Handles Ambiguity: Chooses the most likely correct parse
