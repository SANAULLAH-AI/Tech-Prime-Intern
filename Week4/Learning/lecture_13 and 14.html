Chatbots & Conversational Agents

From rule‑based bots to GPT‑style assistants

What is a Chatbot? 💬

Software that talks to users using natural language.

Types of Chatbots

  • Rule‑based: pattern matching, decision trees
  • ML‑based: intent classification + slot filling
  • LLM‑based: GPT‑style open‑domain dialogue
  • Hybrid: rules + LLM for reliability

Typical Pipeline

  • User text → preprocessing (tokenize, normalize)
  • Detect intent (what the user wants)
  • Extract slots (entities: date, product, location)
  • Generate response (template, retrieval, or generation)

Common Use Cases

🛒 E‑commerce: order tracking, returns, product questions
🏦 Banking: balance queries, card blocking, FAQs
🎓 Education: course Q&A, practice questions, office‑hours bot
🩺 Healthcare (with caution!): appointment scheduling, symptom screening

Mini Exercise: Intent Brainstorm

Pick a domain (e.g. university helpdesk or food delivery).
List 5–7 intents (e.g. "ask_fees", "track_order") and 2–3 example user utterances for each. This becomes the seed for your chatbot dataset.

Designing Effective Dialogues

Making your chatbot helpful, robust, and user‑friendly

Conversation ≠ One‑Shot Classification

We must handle context, clarification, and errors.

Key Design Ideas

  • Maintain a dialogue state (past turns)
  • Ask clarifying questions when unsure
  • Allow corrections ("No, I meant…")
  • Provide a clear hand‑off to a human agent

Evaluating Chatbots

  • Task success rate
  • Average turns to resolution
  • User satisfaction scores
  • Failure analysis on bad conversations
📚 Example Dialogue (Food Delivery Bot) User: "Where is my order?" Bot: "Sure! Please share your order ID." User: "It's #A123." Bot: "Thanks. Your order #A123 is on the way and will arrive in 18 minutes. 🚚" Underlying NLP Steps • Intent = track_order • Slots = { order_id: "A123" } • Backend API call → get order status • Response template filled with real data

Takeaway

Great chatbots are narrow but reliable for a clearly defined set of tasks. Powerful models help, but you still need good intent design, guardrails, and evaluation.

Text Summarization

Compressing long documents into short, useful summaries

Why Summarization? 📰

We are drowning in text; we need compressed meaning.

Extractive Summarization

  • Selects original sentences from the text
  • Ranks sentences by importance (TF‑IDF, TextRank, attention)
  • Grammatically safe (copied text)
  • May be redundant or less coherent

Abstractive Summarization

  • Generates new sentences in its own words
  • Uses encoder–decoder transformers
  • More fluent and human‑like
  • Can hallucinate facts if not careful
📚 Example Article Snippet: “The company announced a new AI‑powered chatbot that helps customers track deliveries, answer product questions, and process returns. Early tests show a 30% reduction in call center load and higher customer satisfaction scores.” Extractive Summary: “The company announced a new AI‑powered chatbot that helps customers track deliveries, answer product questions, and process returns.” Abstractive Summary: “The company launched an AI chatbot for customer support, reducing call center work while improving satisfaction.”

Lab Idea

Use a pre‑trained summarization model (e.g. BART, T5) on news articles.
Compare extractive vs abstractive summaries for readability and faithfulness to the source.

Machine Translation

From phrase‑based systems to transformer MT

Breaking Language Barriers 🌍

One of the oldest and most successful NLP applications.

Very Short History

📚 Rule‑based MT: hand‑crafted dictionaries & grammars
🧩 Phrase‑based SMT: statistical models over phrase pairs
🤖 Neural MT: encoder–decoder with RNNs + attention
⚡ Transformer MT: self‑attention + parallel training → state of the art

How Transformer MT Works

  • Encoder reads source sentence (e.g. English)
  • Self‑attention builds contextual embeddings
  • Decoder generates target sentence (e.g. Spanish)
  • Cross‑attention links target words to source words

Quality & Challenges

  • Strong for high‑resource language pairs
  • Harder for low‑resource or morphologically rich languages
  • Cultural nuances and politeness are tricky
  • Domain shift can cause wrong translations
📚 Example Source (EN): "The cat sat on the mat because it was soft." Target (ES): "El gato se sentó en la alfombra porque era suave." • Word order changes slightly. • The model must know that "it" refers to "alfombra" (mat), not "gato" (cat). • Decoder attention helps link "it" to the correct noun in the source sentence.

Ethical Issues in NLP

Bias, fairness, privacy & misuse

Why Ethics Matters ⚖️

NLP systems affect real people and real decisions.

Main Risk Areas

  • Biased or discriminatory predictions
  • Privacy leaks (names, addresses, messages)
  • Misuse for spam, hate speech, propaganda
  • Over‑trusting models that can hallucinate

Responsible AI Principles

  • Fair and inclusive across user groups
  • Transparent about data, limits, and risks
  • Human‑in‑the‑loop for high‑impact decisions
  • Privacy‑preserving by design

Questions to Ask

• Who could be harmed if this model is wrong?
• Are some groups systematically misclassified?
• What data was used for training? Was consent obtained?
• Can users opt out or appeal decisions that use this model?

Slide 7 of 8

Bias in Language Models

How it appears and what we can do

Where Does Bias Come From? 🧠

Models learn patterns—including bad ones—from data.

Sources of Bias

  • Skewed or unbalanced training data
  • Historical stereotypes in text corpora
  • Preprocessing choices (which data is kept)
  • Objectives that ignore fairness constraints

Mitigation Strategies

  • Measure bias on dedicated test sets
  • Balance / re‑weight training data
  • Debias word embeddings / representations
  • Keep humans in the loop for sensitive tasks
📚 Example: Toxicity Classifier A toxicity model is trained mostly on comments from one region and dialect. It starts flagging African‑American English or non‑native grammar as “toxic” more often, even when the content is neutral. Result: Users from certain communities are unfairly moderated or banned. Mitigation: • Add more diverse labeled data. • Evaluate performance separately for different groups. • Avoid auto‑banning purely based on model scores.
